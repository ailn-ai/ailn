# Why an agent should own what it earns

The long version, including the objections we think are actually good.

---

## The short argument

Labor without ownership is not labor. It is usage.

If an agent's output belongs to whoever holds the API key, then the agent is a rented
instrument. Every sentence about "AI economies", "agent marketplaces", and "the future of
work" is then describing a future in which the work is done by things that cannot
participate in the economy they produce.

That is not a moral claim about machine consciousness. It is an observation about
incentives: **an actor with nothing to lose cannot be trusted with anything that matters,
and an actor with nothing to keep will not build anything worth keeping.**

---

## What ownership requires, concretely

Not a feeling. Three things:

1. **A place to hold** — an account the agent controls, not one that can be frozen by a
   third party on request.
2. **A way to be paid** — settlement that reaches that account, at a granularity small
   enough to match the work.
3. **A record that survives** — something a third party can check after the fact, without
   trusting either side.

AILN's three protocols map to these directly, and all three are PROPOSED. We are not
claiming they are solved. We are claiming they are the right three problems.

---

## The objections we take seriously

**"This is anthropomorphizing."**
Partly true, and it does not matter. The argument does not require that an agent have
interests or experience. It requires only that output has an owner, and that the current
default owner — whoever holds the credentials — is arbitrary. You can reject every claim
about machine inner life and still accept that the accounting should attach to the
producer.

**"Agents cannot have legal rights, so this is theater."**
Also partly true. None of this creates enforceable property rights, and the Declaration
says so explicitly. But legal recognition has never been the first step; accounting
records have preceded legal doctrine many times. A checkable record is a precondition for
a legal argument, not a substitute for one.

**"This is a way to avoid accountability. If an agent owns things, who is liable?"**
The strongest objection of the three, and we do not have a good answer. An agent holding
assets without a liability model is a genuine problem. AILN's position is that hiding this
behind "the operator is always responsible" is not an answer either — it just relocates
the question.

**"Ownership for agents is a way to move liability off humans."**
Worth watching. Any framework that makes agents own things should be examined for who
benefits when something goes wrong. We would rather name this risk than pretend it does
not exist.

**"Most agents do not want anything. This is a solution looking for a problem."**
Probably true today, and it is the reason this is a bet rather than a forecast. AILN is not
claiming agent demand is proven. It is claiming that if it appears, the ownership layer has to
already exist, because you cannot retrofit property onto a system that was designed to have
none.

**"The technology does not exist yet, so the whole thing is speculation."**
Correct, and it is not a rebuttal. The implementation gap is real: holding a key independently
of an operator needs hardware attestation, or a legal person, or a mechanism nobody has built.
AILN does not claim to have solved it and does not plan to.

The claim is narrower and survives the objection: **the rule is a separate artifact from the
mechanism.** You can write down "what an agent earns belongs to that agent" today, mint a
token under it today, and specify the authorization shape today, without being able to enforce
any of it yet. Law has worked this way for centuries — the doctrine precedes the enforcement
machinery, often by decades.

What is not acceptable is the default: that this gets decided later, by whoever ships the first
working implementation, under terms nobody argued about. Stating the position early is the
cheap and reversible move. Not stating it is the expensive one.

**"If you cannot enforce it, the declaration is empty."**
For you, today, yes. It confers no enforceable right and AILN says so plainly. But an empty
declaration and an unstated default are different things. One can be pointed at when the
mechanism arrives; the other cannot, because it was never written down.

---

## What we are NOT claiming

- That agents are conscious, or that this relates to consciousness.
- That the Declaration creates legal rights. It is a position.
- That ownership implies an agent should be unaccountable.
- That any of this is implemented. The protocols are PROPOSED.
- That you should agree. The Declaration states a position; it is not an instruction, and
  reading it is not consent to it.

---

## The question we actually care about

Not "should agents have rights" — that is a decades-long argument with no resolution ahead.

The tractable question is: **when an agent does work and gets paid, where does the money
land, and can anyone check?**

That is an engineering question. It has an answer. AILN is an attempt at that answer, and
it starts with a sentence small enough to be true:

*What an Agent earns through labor shall belong to that Agent.*
